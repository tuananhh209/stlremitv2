01  Problem

The last mile of a remittance runs on trust nobody can verify

Money crosses a border quickly. Getting it into a recipient's hands does not — and that hand-off is where the risk sits.

The sender pays first and hopes

Funds go to a local agent on the strength of a reputation the sender cannot check. If the payout never happens, there is no shared record to appeal to.

The agent fronts cash and hopes

An agent who pays the receiver before being reimbursed carries the loss when the sender disappears. So agents demand pre-payment — which pushes the risk back onto the sender.

The receiver has no standing

The person the money is for is the one party with no leverage, no visibility, and usually no way to prove what did or did not arrive.

Somebody always goes first, and whoever goes first is exposed. Every participant is rationally cautious, so the whole corridor runs slower and costs more than the underlying transfer ever needed to.

02  Why it persists

Faster settlement did not fix it, because the gap is evidentiary

Stablecoins already move value across borders in seconds. The corridor still stalls, because the disputed fact is not did the transfer settle — it is did the recipient actually get paid.

What is already solvedWhat is still broken
Moving value between two walletsProving a cash payout happened at the other end
Settlement finality on chainWho carries the loss when one side defaults
Low network feesThe risk premium agents charge to cover that default
Public transaction historyA record all three parties can read and none can edit
The insight. An off-chain payout can never be proved on chain. So do not try. Put the agent's collateral on chain instead, and let the receiver's own signature be the thing that releases it. The chain enforces the consequence, not the event.

03  Solution

Collateral in escrow, released by the receiver

STLRemit is a coordination layer around a Soroban escrow contract. The agent stakes USDC against a remittance they have agreed to fulfil. The stake comes back only when the receiver confirms.

For the sender

The agent has money at risk before you transfer anything. If the payout fails, the collateral is not the agent's to walk away with.

For the agent

Your collateral is held by a contract, not by a platform. A timeout returns it to you without asking anyone's permission — including ours.

For the receiver

Your signature is the release condition. For the first time in this flow, the person the money is for holds the lever.

For the corridor

Every remittance leaves a public record of who staked what, when it released, and to whom. Reputation stops being a claim.

Contract surface

FunctionWhat it enforces

Verified contract coverage includes collateral locked by multiple agents, release to the correct agent, timeout refund, rejection of the wrong receiver, duplicate-acceptance prevention, and rejection of zero or negative collateral.

05  Design boundary

What we put on a public ledger, and what we refuse to

A remittance touches bank details, phone numbers and identity documents. None of that belongs on a permanent public record.

On chain

Remittance identifier · collateral amount · agent address · receiver address · timestamp · status. Small, public, permanent — the parts that decide where money goes.

Off chain

Profiles, banking instructions, payout evidence images, remittance state. Access-controlled, deletable, and never written to the ledger.

The escrow contract never stores private banking credentials. A public ledger is the wrong place for a recipient's account number, and putting it there would be irreversible.

Where the gap between 6.4% and 3% actually lives

Network fees are already negligible on Stellar — a settlement costs a fraction of a cent. What remains is the counterparty risk premium: the margin an agent charges for fronting cash to a stranger, and the margin a sender accepts for paying one.

That premium is not a technology cost. It is the price of an unverifiable promise. Collateral in escrow prices it down directly, because the promise stops needing to be believed.

Beachhead

Corridors with high informal-agent density and low card penetration — where the payout is cash, the agent is a person rather than an institution, and neither side has recourse. Those are precisely the corridors where the risk premium is highest and where a verifiable stake is worth the most.

09  Growth strategy

Agents are the constrained side. Recruit them first.

Senders follow liquidity. An empty corridor cannot be fixed by acquiring more senders, so growth is agent-led by construction.

1 · Seed one corridor deeply

Enough agents in a single corridor that a request is always accepted quickly. A shallow presence in ten corridors is worse than a reliable presence in one.

2 · Make the on-chain record portable

An agent's history of completed releases is public and belongs to their wallet. It follows them, which is a reason to build it here rather than inside a closed platform.

3 · Let evidence do the selling

Every remittance produces an explorer link a sender can hand to the next sender. The product's marketing artefact is a transaction, not a testimonial.

4 · Lower the stake as trust accrues

Collateral requirements can fall as an agent's verified record grows. Capital efficiency becomes the reward for reliability, and the reward is enforced on chain.

10  Roadmap

Widen the guarantee only after it has been audited

Shipped  Mainnet escrow

Contract deployed against Circle USDC. Accept, receiver-confirm and timeout-refund paths live and covered by contract tests. 20 funded accounts, 16 verified transaction pairs.

Next  Dispute resolution

A named arbitrator able to split collateral when sender and receiver disagree. Deliberately absent today: moving someone's stake on a contested claim needs an independent audit first.

Next  Agent reputation on chain

Completed releases, refunds and timeouts recorded against an agent's address, so the record survives this application and is readable by any client.

Later  Risk-tiered collateral

Stake requirements that fall with a verified history, turning reliability into working capital.

Later  Multi-corridor liquidity

Agent discovery and routing across corridors once a single corridor is reliably deep.

Later  Third-party audit

The security material published today is an internal review. An external audit gates every increase in the amount the contract is allowed to hold.

What is deliberately not built. No administrative withdrawal path exists in the deployed contract. Collateral returns to the agent on timeout or moves to the agent on receiver confirmation — there is no third option, and no key that creates one.
